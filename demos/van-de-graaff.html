Suppose the dome is already at $150\text{ kV}$ and the belt carries more positive charge up inside the hollow dome. Will that charge be repelled back down, or flow onto the dome? What changes if you make the dome larger at the same air gap?

Show the answer

It still flows onto the dome! Inside a hollow conductor, charges on the outer surface produce zero electric field (Faraday's ice-pail effect), so new charge at the upper comb always moves outward. A larger dome has greater capacitance $C = 4\pi\varepsilon_0 R$, so it stores more charge at the same voltage and takes longer to recharge between sparks.

  • Charge delivered inside the hollow shell moves immediately to the outer surface, so $V(t)$ keeps climbing linearly until leakage or breakdown intervenes.
  • A wider spark gap $d$ raises the breakdown voltage $V_{\text{spark}}$, giving less frequent but much more energetic sparks ($U = \tfrac{1}{2}CV^2$).
  • Switch Air to Humid: air leakage grows with $V$, curving the voltage graph. If leakage equals belt current before $V_{\text{spark}}$, the voltage levels off and sparks stop.
Common mistake. People often expect the dome to "push back" on new charge once its voltage is high. That happens if you try to add charge to the outside, not when a belt delivers charge to a collector comb inside a conducting shell.

An isolated conducting sphere of radius $R$ has capacitance $C$ and stores energy $U$:

$$C = 4\pi\varepsilon_0 R,\qquad V(t) = \frac{Q(t)}{C},\qquad E_{\text{surf}} = \frac{V}{R},\qquad U = \tfrac{1}{2}CV^2$$

With belt charging current $I_{\text{belt}}$ and effective air leakage resistance $R_{\text{leak}}$, the dome charge evolves as:

$$\frac{dQ}{dt} = I_{\text{belt}} - \frac{V}{R_{\text{leak}}},\qquad V_{\text{spark}} \approx E_{\text{crit}}\frac{R\,d}{R + d}$$

Model assumptions: isolated-sphere capacitance, lumped ohmic air leakage, and a smooth sphere-gap breakdown approximation interpolating between a small uniform gap ($E_{\text{crit}}d$) and an isolated sphere corona limit ($E_{\text{crit}}R$).
